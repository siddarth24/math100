2.1.1 — Calculating limits with limit laws

Find limits from formulas: plug the number in first, and if you get 00\frac{0}{0}00​ (a hole in disguise), factor or rationalize to cancel the zero-maker.

The intuition: walking toward a point on the graph

Imagine walking along a curve toward x=ax = ax=a from both the left and the right. A limit only asks: what height yyy are your feet heading toward as you get right next to x=ax = ax=a? It does not care what happens at the exact spot x=ax = ax=a itself—there can be a hole or a displaced dot and the limit stays the same. A function is continuous at x=ax = ax=a when there is no surprise: the left path, the right path, and the dot f(a)f(a)f(a) all meet at the exact same height.

  • "Evaluate lim⁡x→af(x)\lim_{x \to a} f(x)limx→a​f(x)" → trace the curve from both sides of aaa. If both sides head to the same height, that number is your answer. If they head to different heights, say "does not exist (DNE)."
  • "x→a−x \to a^-x→a− vs. x→a+x \to a^+x→a+" → a−a^-a− means walk in from the left only (numbers slightly smaller than aaa, like 2.992.992.99 for 3−3^-3−); a+a^+a+ means walk in from the right only (like 3.013.013.01 for 3+3^+3+).
  • "Find kkk so that fff is continuous" → plug the seam point into the left formula and the right formula, then set the two results equal so the pieces connect without a jump.
  • "Horizontal vs. vertical asymptote" → a horizontal asymptote is the height the graph flattens out to far to the left or right (x→±∞x \to \pm\inftyx→±∞); a vertical asymptote is an xxx-value where the graph shoots straight up or down to ±∞\pm\infty±∞ (usually where a denominator hits 000).
